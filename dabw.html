Designing the AfroVerse with Black Women in Computing

A pop-up, Black feminist makerspace where Black women designed Afrofuturist e-textile technology, and what it taught us about building spaces where people feel safe enough to make.

My role
Lead researcher and facilitator
Team
Emory research team directed by Dr. Yolanda Rankin
Timeline
Spring 2025 to Spring 2026
Methods
Sister circles, PAR-informed design, qualitative analysis

The challenge

Makerspaces promise to widen who builds technology but often reproduce exclusion. Black women in computing needed a space safe enough to ask, try and build.

What I did

Co-led a year-long study end to end: planning and the IRB protocol, facilitator training on four fabrication tools, sister-circle design workshops and qualitative analysis.

The outcome

Participants gained technical skills, belonging and interest in tech. Findings became four design commitments other HCI teams can use.

The problem

Makerspaces promise to broaden who gets to build technology. In practice, they often reproduce the same exclusionary power structures and masculine norms as the rest of tech.

For Black women in computing, that compounds what research already shows about CS classrooms: they can be hostile places to learn. And when you're new to the tools, it's hard to even know what question to ask.

We also had no permanent room and no budget for one. Whatever we built had to travel.

How might we design a makerspace where Black women feel safe enough to ask, try, fail and build?

Research questions

RQ1

How can we apply Black Feminist Epistemologies to create a makerspace that affirms the intersectional identities of women of the African diaspora?

RQ2

Using sister circles as the underlying infrastructure, what is the impact of the makerspace on its Black women facilitators and participants?

My approach

Sister circles as infrastructure
Sister circles are an established method for doing research with Black women. We extended them past dialogue into design and making, so the circle became the place where participants critiqued, imagined and built.
Afrofuturism as the lens
A form of speculative design that positions Black people as inventors of technology. It gave participants permission to design for futures that center them.
A pop-up space
Designed to be transient, flexible and periodic. Portability made it reachable for women with transportation, financial or space limits.
Shared authority
Participants shaped the workshop design and the final presentation of findings. Facilitators sat beside participants, not in front of them.

The workshops

The team chose an e-textiles theme because it starts from crafts many women already know and adapts to every skill level. Participants rotated through four technologies:

Cricut Maker 4

Precision cutting for fabric and vinyl

LilyPad Arduino

Sewable electronics and circuits

Embroidery machine

Digital pattern stitching

3D printer

Rapid prototyping of hard parts

Each participant picked a design prompt, defined a problem from her own life, wrote user stories ("As a user, I want to ___ so that I can ___"), sketched ideas, then built a prototype.

One build, start to finish

Imani's shirt shows the full arc a participant took, from an idea on the whiteboard to a garment that responds to its surroundings.

She sketched a tree rooted in a meditating Black woman, a design she connected to the chronically invisible labor that sustains communities. She cut the layers on the Cricut, heat-pressed them onto a shirt, then built a sewable circuit on the back.

A photoresistor reads the room. When it gets dark, the LEDs light one after another, like a walkway:

// When the room is dark, light the LEDs in sequence
lightValue = analogRead(sensorPin);
if (lightValue > darkLevel) {
  digitalWrite(ledPinA, HIGH); delay(1000);
  digitalWrite(ledPin2, HIGH); delay(1000);
  digitalWrite(ledPin3, HIGH); delay(1000);
  digitalWrite(ledPin4, HIGH); delay(1000);
  digitalWrite(ledPin1, HIGH);
}

What we learned

Welcoming and safe are not the same thing

Participants described tech spaces that looked open but made asking questions feel risky. DABW felt different because no one had to already know the vocabulary to get help.

"So for me, this was welcoming, but tech overall, no. Because I feel like if you don't know what you're doing, it's very hard to ask."Diamond, participant

From consumers to creators

Even participants with CS degrees found new ways into computing once it met craft.

"I have more positive feelings about computing… I didn't realize that it merged so nicely with e-textiles and like 3D printing and like all these things."Jayla, participant

Hands-on rotation built real skills

Moving between machines gave participants guided time on tools they wanted to learn for their own lives.

"I enjoyed like the rotation of learning how to use different machines… especially the sewing machine because I'm trying to get into sewing."Danielle, participant

Design became counternarrative

Given full control, participants designed for Black hair care, skin care, fashion and social justice. One designed a peaceful landscape of the Democratic Republic of Congo, linking the cobalt in our electronics to a conflict rarely discussed.

"So this could be what Congo could look like if all that stuff wasn't happening."Diamond, participant

People stayed

No one abandoned their project, even through difficulties. On several occasions, facilitators had to ask participants to leave because they kept working past the session.

Impact

Across participants and facilitators, Black women:

Acquired new technical skills
Felt they belonged
Co-created community
Grew their interest in tech

Design commitments for HCI

What other researchers and designers can take from DABW:

  1. Treat welcoming and safe as separate design targetsA space can signal welcome and still feel unsafe. Design and evaluate for both.
  2. Build multiple entry points into makingStart from skills people already hold, like sewing, then route them into circuits and fabrication.
  3. Offer physical and temporal flexibilityDrop-in sessions, office hours and a portable space made room for real schedules.
  4. Design for restraint, not just presenceFacilitators shared power on purpose, so participants' knowledge could lead.

What I'd change next time

  • Budget more time for machine troubleshooting. Our workshop equipment differed from the units we trained on.
  • Lengthen sessions or add machines. Three-hour sessions and limited equipment constrained mastery, and possibly which projects people chose.
  • Give Arduino more room. Participants said it was the technology they explored least.
  • Separate attendance from compensation. Payment was tied to 75% attendance, so we can't attribute sustained attendance purely to attachment to the space.
