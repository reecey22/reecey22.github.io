Accessibility research

Encodings: Reading Data Without Sight

Research study, Emory University, Summer 2023

This study builds on Let's Get Vysical: Perceptual Accuracy in Visual and Tactile Encodings (Xu, Williams and Wall, 2023), which adapted Cleveland and McGill's classic graphical perception study to compare how accurately people read data from visual and tactile encodings.

Our web experiment showed the same kinds of comparisons in three formats: visual encodings, plain tables, and enhanced tables that describe each shape's position and size. Participants worked through examples, practice rounds and trials for each format, then a short survey.

My role
Redesigned the enhanced tables after finding the original format far too complex for navigating data. Ran the study and led recruitment of participants who are blind or visually impaired.
Methods
Online perception experiment, examples and practice trials, participant survey, recruitment with blind and visually impaired communities
Outcome
Feedback from blind and visually impaired participants pointed to several weaknesses in our approach: the table-based encodings were not very beneficial to them.

The biggest lesson: test with the people a tool is meant for as early as possible. Their feedback showed problems we couldn't see from the inside.

VR and game design

The FINal Straw: A VR Game About Goldfish Care

Team project, two-week design sprint, Spring 2024

A mobile VR game about how not to take care of a goldfish, made for a Shinecon headset and controller. Players live three days as a goldfish and face what happens when its owner gets the basics wrong, from algae overgrowth to the wrong plants.

My role
Interaction developer: controller and keyboard input, camera rotation, scene reloading and the "You Died" end screens
Team
Four-person team; I contributed the interaction code
Tools
A-Frame (WebXR), JavaScript, HTML, 3D models and audio

Physical computing

Heads or Tails: An Arduino Guessing Game

Solo project, Physical Computing midterm, Fall 2024

A two-button coin-flip game on an Arduino and an 8×8 LED matrix. Press the left button for heads or the right for tails; the code flips a random coin and the matrix answers with a smiley face for a right guess or a sad face for a wrong one.

My role
Solo designer and developer: circuit, code, custom LED bitmaps and playtesting
Tools
Arduino, MAX7219 LED matrix, push buttons, Adafruit GFX library
Playtesting
With no instructions on the device, the player was confused at first, then worked out the controls by pressing buttons and watching the display. After that the game felt straightforward and the buttons satisfying to press, and the player started testing whether the "random" flips followed a pattern.

Even a two-button game needs a way to tell people how to play. The first press shouldn't be a guess.
