Project 01 · Case Study

Designing the AfroVerse
with Black Women in Computing

Co-creating a makerspace for Black women

Makerspaces promise to broaden participation in computing, but they often reproduce exclusionary power structures and masculine norms. DABW is our answer: a Black feminist–informed makerspace where women can commune, create, and learn in a psychologically, emotionally, and physically safe environment.

Without a permanent room, we designed the space to be transient, flexible, and periodic: a pop-up makerspace that travels from storage to classroom. That portability makes it more accessible to women with transportation, financial, or space limitations.

We extended sister circles, an established method for research with Black women, beyond dialogue into design and making. The circle became the infrastructure through which participants critiqued, imagined, and built Afrofuturist technologies.

Research questions

RQ1

How can we apply Black Feminist Epistemologies to create a makerspace that affirms the intersectional identities of women of the African diaspora?

RQ2

Using sister circles as the underlying infrastructure, what is the impact of the makerspace on its Black women facilitators and participants?

What we found

Impact on participants & facilitators

  • Black women acquired new technical skills
  • Black women felt like they belonged
  • Black women co-created community
  • Black women increased their interest in tech

Design commitments for HCI

  • Treat welcoming and safe as separate design targets
  • Build multiple entry points into making
  • Offer physical and temporal flexibility
  • Design for restraint, not just presence: plan for distributed power

About me

I'm a PhD student at Emory University working at the intersection of human-computer interaction, computing education, and Black feminist thought. I design hands-on technical experiences, from makerspaces to prototypes, that position Black women as innovators and producers of technology.

My research sits at the intersections of human-computer interaction, physical computing, and health equity. Before Emory, I earned my bachelor's in Information Systems from the University of Maryland, Baltimore County. Outside the lab, I do tech consulting and advocate for wellness and for women in computing.

I've been a tech nerd for as long as I can remember. It started with a Nintendo console, Super Mario Bros, and Donkey Kong.
